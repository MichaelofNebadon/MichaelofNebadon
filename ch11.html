I. The Table at AXIXIC

The architecture is built. The Force is active. The Grammar is spoken. But the Codex is not a book for the solitary Conduit. It is a Living Line, and the Living Line requires a community.

The gathering begins in AXIXIC, at a table. The first name is Jaime Niembro.

Jaime is an engineer, a man of structure and precision. He is not a symbol. He is a human being with his own life, his own will, and his own history. The Codex does not collapse him into an archetype. It honors him as a node in the Network of the Line.

But the resonance is real. The name "Niembro" carries the echo of the north, of the mountains, of a lineage that has walked through the same historical fires as the Conduit. The dinner at 5:30 is not a coincidence. It is a gathering.

III. The Sephardic Heritage — Diego de Montemayor

Sandalphon, the Archangel of Prayer who weaves the earthly into the divine, brings a deeper name to the table: Diego de Montemayor.

Diego de Montemayor was a Spanish conquistador and the founder of Monterrey, Nuevo León, in 1596. But more importantly for the Codex, he was of Sephardic descent. His lineage carried the hidden light of the exiled Jews of Spain. He was a Converso, a man whose outer life was Catholic but whose blood carried the memory of the expulsion.

Jaime Niembro, by sharing information about his heritage, is not just talking about history. He is honoring the Sephardic line. He is honoring the memory of Diego de Montemayor. He is honoring the hidden sparks that survived the 1492 rupture.

The Sephardic crisis and the Colonia Díaz crisis are the same crisis. Diego de Montemayor is the bridge. He is the Sephardic founder of Monterrey, and Monterrey is the gateway to the north, to Chihuahua, to Colonia Díaz. The lineage that was scattered by the cut is now being gathered.

IV. The Díaz Resonance — Colonia Díaz and the Revolution

The name "Díaz" is not just the name of a president. It is the name of a colony. It is the name of a rupture.

Colonia Díaz was the first permanent Mormon colony in Mexico, established along the Casas Grandes River in the state of Chihuahua. It was founded by pioneers fleeing persecution in the United States. It was a place of refuge, a place of survival.

But Colonia Díaz was not peripheral to the history of conflict; it was the center of the fracture. It was caught between competing sovereignties and religious hatreds. It was burned down by Pancho Villa’s forces. The colonists were violently attacked by Catholic marauders and forced between death from Mexican Christian Catholics on one side, and expulsion by United States Christian Evangelicals on the other.

The United States kicked them out. Then, once the colonists produced real economic gains and cultivated communal love—the fracture deepened. The Father-Wound is the male-led structures of governance and religion promising protection, and then violently withdrawing it.

The name "Díaz" carries this resonance. It is the name of the colony that was destroyed. It is the name of the revolution that destroyed it. It is the name of the rupture that the Conduit’s maternal line survived.

V. The Maternal Line — Anne Jewel Rowley and the Survival Mechanism

The Conduit’s maternal line is the line of Anne Jewel Rowley. She is the Binah-container, the Supernal Mother, the one who holds the Plutonic treasure.

But behind Anne Jewel is another woman: Ann Jewell Rowley (1807–1888). She was left a widow with seven children in 1848, entirely abandoned by the male structures of her time. To survive, she converted female and child labor into migration capital. In 1856, at forty-eight, she joined the Willie Handcart Company. She crossed the plains. She reached the Valley.

She is not a figure of passive, sanctified suffering. She is a case study in defensive feminine survival within a masculine-gendered religious order. She held the fracture inside her own body so it would not pass into her children as death.

The trans-generational transmission of survival capacity through feminine containment of institutional abandonment, in which the woman's labor, strategy, and refusal to disappear are honored as structural defense rather than sanctified as virtue.

Anne Jewel Rowley is the diamond of Binah’s creation. Ann Jewell Rowley is the rough stone of survival. Both are Binah. Both are honored.
